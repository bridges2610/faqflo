You’ve spent years asking customers to leave a review. You’ve got 180 of them and a 4.8 average. So
when someone asks ChatGPT for the best plumber in your town, surely that’s the thing that wins it,
right?

Sort of. Reviews matter. They’re just not doing the job you think they’re doing.

Here’s the short version: reviews help an AI decide between businesses it already knows about. They
rarely get you into the conversation in the first place. Those are two different problems, and most
business owners are only solving one of them.

## What AI actually sees when it looks at you

When someone asks an AI assistant for a local recommendation, it’s pulling from a few different
places at once.

**Your Google Business Profile.** Your name, category, address, phone, hours, and star rating. This
is the backbone of local AI answers — Google’s AI Overviews sits directly on top of it, and other
assistants lean on similar business listing data. If your profile is thin, wrong, or unclaimed,
you’re already behind.

**Review text — sometimes, and in pieces.** Assistants don’t read all 180 of your reviews. They tend
to work from summaries, star ratings, and whatever review snippets happen to be visible on pages
they can crawl. So a common theme across many reviews (“always on time,” “didn’t upsell me”) is more
likely to surface than any single glowing review.

**Third-party sites.** Yelp, Angi, Nextdoor, Reddit threads, “best X in [town]” listicles from local
blogs. These get crawled and quoted constantly, often more than your own website.

**Your own website — if it can read it.** This is the part almost everyone gets wrong, and we’ll
come back to it.

## Where reviews genuinely help

Let’s be fair to reviews, because they do real work.

**They’re a trust filter.** A business with 8 reviews and a 3.9 average is less likely to get named
than one with 200 and a 4.8. Ratings are one of the few hard numbers an AI can compare across
businesses, so it uses them.

**Review language becomes recommendation language.** When an AI says “known for fast response times
and fair pricing,” it often got that phrasing from patterns in your reviews. Your customers are, in
a real sense, writing your AI description for you.

**They’re the second look.** Even in a zero-click world, plenty of people take the name the AI gave
them and search it before calling. Your reviews are what they find. The AI got you the shot; your
reviews close it.

## Where reviews don’t help at all

Now the uncomfortable part.

**Reviews don’t answer questions.** Nobody’s review says “yes, they service Lakewood and Golden, and
there’s no after-hours surcharge.” But that’s exactly what the customer asked the AI. If nowhere on
the readable web states your service area, your pricing model, or your response time, the AI can’t
say it about you — no matter how many stars you have.

**Reviews are on Google’s property, not yours.** You don’t control that page, you can’t add detail
to it, and it’s structured for humans browsing, not for an engine extracting specifics.

**Reviews can’t be your only crawlable content.** If your website is a beautiful,
[JavaScript-heavy brochure](/blog/why-faq-plugins-arent-a-good-idea) with no plain text an AI can
read, then your Google profile is doing all the work by itself. That’s enough to appear in a list.
It’s not enough to be the one that gets recommended, because there’s nothing specific for the AI to
say about you.

Here’s the pattern we see constantly: a business with excellent reviews gets mentioned as a name in
a list, while a competitor with worse reviews gets the actual recommendation — because the
competitor’s site answers the question and theirs doesn’t.

## The fix is boring and it works

Do both. They’re not in competition.

**Keep your Google Business Profile perfect.** Claimed, correct category, current hours, correct
service area, photos, and the Q&A section on the profile actually filled in. That last one is
skipped by nearly everyone and it’s free.

**Keep asking for reviews.** And when you do, nudge people toward specifics. “If you have a second,
it helps if you mention what we did and how fast we got there.” A review that says what you did is
worth ten that say “great service.”

**Then put the answers on your own site as plain, readable text.** The questions customers actually
ask — what it costs, which towns you cover, how fast you can come out, what you don’t do. In real
HTML, on your domain, readable without JavaScript. That’s the material an AI builds a recommendation
out of, and it’s the only part of this you fully control.

This is where regular SEO and AEO sit next to each other, by the way. A healthy website and a solid
Google profile are the foundation — they always were.
[Answer Engine Optimization](/blog/what-is-aeo) is the floor built on top: making sure the specifics
of your business exist somewhere an answer engine can lift them from.
