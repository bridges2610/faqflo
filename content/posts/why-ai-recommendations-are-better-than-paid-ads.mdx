Meta and Google will sell you clicks all day. Neither one can sell you the sentence where AI names
your business.

<PostTldr>

Picture the same customer three times.

**On Instagram**, your ad slides past between a friend’s vacation photos and a recipe video. They
weren’t thinking about their water heater. You paid for that moment anyway.

**On Google**, they search “emergency plumber Denver.” Your ad is at the top, marked Ad. So are three
competitors. Whoever bid most is above you, and every click costs you money whether or not the phone
rings.

**In ChatGPT**, they type, “who’s good for emergency plumbing in Denver?” The AI names three
businesses. Yours is one of them, with a sentence about why. They call.

Same customer, same problem, three completely different conversations. Only one of them arrives with
your name already vouched for.

## What you’re really buying from Meta

Meta ads are interruption, and that’s not an insult — it’s the product. Facebook and Instagram are
very good at putting your business in front of people who match a description. Homeowners.
Thirty-five and up. Ten miles from your shop.

But matching a description isn’t the same as needing you. Nobody opens Instagram hoping to find a
plumber. So you pay to reach a hundred people on the chance that one of them has a problem today,
and you pay again tomorrow to do it over.

That’s why Meta works better for some businesses than others. If what you sell is impulse-friendly
or visual, interruption can pay. If what you sell is “my basement is flooding right now,” you’re
buying a lot of scrolls to find one emergency.

## What you’re really buying from Google

Google ads solve the intent problem. That person typed the words. They want a plumber, right now, in
your city. That’s a far better starting point than a scroll.

The catch is the auction. You’re not paying what the click is worth — you’re paying what your most
aggressive competitor is willing to pay for the same click. In the trades that make good money per
job, that’s a bidding war between people who all know the job is worth it.

So costs climb, your margin is set by somebody else’s budget, and the moment you pause the campaign
you vanish. You never accumulate anything. Year three of running Google ads doesn’t cost less than
year one.

And there’s a newer wrinkle: on a lot of searches, Google now answers the question itself in an AI
Overview before the results even start. Your ad still shows. It’s just no longer the first thing on
the page.

## The part neither of them sells

Here’s the honest correction, because this changed recently and plenty of advice hasn’t caught up:
you can buy ads inside AI tools now. ChatGPT started showing sponsored placements in 2026, and
Google runs ads in and around AI Overviews — bought through the same Google Ads account you already
have.

But look closely at what that money buys. It buys a block labeled Sponsored, sitting beside or below
the answer, clearly separated from it. It’s the same ad you’ve always bought, in a new room. Both
companies have been clear that advertisers don’t get to influence what the AI actually says.

The recommendation itself is not for sale. The part where the AI says your business name, in its own
voice, as the answer — there’s no bid for that. You get named because the engine found clear,
specific, credible information about what you do and where you do it, and decided you were a good
fit.

Which means a competitor can’t outspend you into that spot. They can outbid you on Google all day
and buy every sponsored card underneath the answer. They cannot write a bigger check to be the
business ChatGPT names on a Sunday morning when someone’s water heater is leaking.

For a small business, that’s rare. Meta and Google both reward whoever has the deepest pockets. This
one rewards whoever is clearest.

## The trust is on a different level

Your customers have spent twenty years learning to skip ads. The little “Sponsored” or “Ad” label is
doing quiet damage every time — it tells the reader this business paid to be here, and the reader
adjusts.

That gap is wider than ever now that both things share a screen. The AI gives its answer, and a
sponsored card sits right underneath. The reader can see exactly which businesses were chosen and
which ones bought their way in.

An AI recommendation carries none of that baggage. It doesn’t read as a purchase. It reads as an
answer.

And the AI doesn’t just list you — it says why. “Flat monthly pricing, no onboarding fee.” “Licensed
techs, no after-hours surcharge.” That’s not a directory listing. That’s the sentence a friend says
when they recommend somebody.

By the time that customer calls, half the selling is done. They’re not asking who you are or whether
you’re legitimate. They’re asking when you can come out.

## They’re not cold — they asked the question

This is the difference you feel in your actual day, on the phone.

A Meta lead is the coldest of the three. You created the interest, so you also have to explain who
you are, prove you’re real, and handle the fact that they were mid-scroll a minute ago.

A Google lead is warm — they were looking. But they clicked an ad, they probably clicked two others,
and they’re comparing you on price before you’ve said a word.

An AI recommendation arrives differently:

- **Real need.** They have the problem right now.
- **Real intent.** They went looking, in their own words.
- **Short list.** They got two or three names, not ten ads and a map.
- **Less haggling.** They came from a recommendation, not a comparison page.

Being one of three recommended businesses is a different position than being the fourth ad on a
results page. You’re not competing for attention. You’re competing to answer the phone.

## When you stop paying, it stops

This is the one that matters most over a few years.

Meta and Google are rent. The leads arrive while the card is being charged and stop the day it
isn’t. Pause for a slow month and you don’t get a smaller trickle — you get nothing. Nothing you
paid for last year is still working for you today.

Answers you publish on your own website behave the opposite way. They sit there. They get read at
2am, in towns where nobody’s heard of you, on a weekend, with no budget attached. You’re not renting
the spot. You’re building the thing that earns it.

## The catch: the AI has to be able to see you

Here’s the part that quietly sinks most small businesses.

An AI can only recommend a business it can read something about. When it can’t find anything useful
about you, it doesn’t say “I’m not sure.” It confidently names somebody else.

Silence isn’t neutral. When an AI can’t see you, the recommendation goes to your competitor.

This trips up owners who think they’ve handled it. You may have a lovely FAQ page. But
[most FAQ plugins and accordion widgets](/blog/why-faq-plugins-arent-a-good-idea) build their
answers with JavaScript, and AI crawlers generally don’t run JavaScript. Your answers look perfect
to a visitor and are invisible to the engines you’re trying to reach.

To be recommended, your answers have to exist as plain, crawlable HTML on your own website — text
that’s in the page from the first byte, with no JavaScript needed to reveal it. That’s the version
an answer engine can read, trust, and quote back with credit to you.

## This doesn’t mean turn off your ads

To be clear: if your Meta or Google campaigns are profitable, keep running them. This is another
channel, not a replacement, and it doesn’t fill your calendar next Tuesday the way a Google ad can.

The same goes for the SEO work you’ve already paid for. None of it is wasted — a healthy, crawlable
site is exactly what makes an AI comfortable recommending you.

Think of it as a building. SEO is the foundation, still holding everything up.
[AEO — answer engine optimization](/blog/what-is-aeo) — is the next floor, and it’s the one going up
right now while most small businesses aren’t looking.

The difference is what you’re buying. Meta and Google rent you attention. Answers you publish keep
working after the invoice stops.
