## Watch a 19-year-old find a mechanic

Picture someone in their late teens or early twenties. Their car is making a noise. They don’t open a
search engine and type “auto repair near me.” They tap the little microphone in ChatGPT and say, “Who
can look at a rattling noise in my car today, somewhere close?”

They get an answer in a few seconds. Two or three names, a sentence about each. They pick one.

No results page. No scrolling. No landing on your homepage. Just a question, spoken out loud, and an
answer.

That’s not a fringe habit. It’s how a growing share of younger people look for everything — a
dentist, a taco place, a tutor, a plumber. And the ones doing it now are the ones who will be buying
homes, running households, and hiring services for the next ten years.

## Young people didn’t switch to AI. They started there.

If you’ve been in business a while, you learned to be found in a certain order. First the phone book,
then the search engine, then reviews, then social. Each one was a layer you added.

Your youngest customers skipped most of that. Many of them opened an AI assistant before they ever
got in the habit of “Googling it.” Pew’s 2026 survey of Americans and AI found 18-to-29-year-olds are
the heaviest chatbot users of any age group, at around two-thirds, and a January 2026 Search Engine
Land study put the share of consumers who now start a search with AI rather than Google at 37%.

It isn’t a clean sweep — some trackers have Gen X adopting chatbots faster than younger adults on
certain measures — but the direction among the youngest adults is not in much doubt.

That matters for one simple reason. Habits formed at 18 don’t reset at 30. The way someone finds a
business in their first years of adulthood is the way they’ll find one for decades. You’re not just
competing for this year’s customer. You’re competing for the default in that person’s head.

## Voice changes what a “question” looks like

Here’s the part that should change how you write.

Typing makes people terse. You’d type “emergency dentist Boston.” Speaking makes people normal. You’d
say, “Is there a dentist near me that can see me today for a broken tooth?”

The difference is measurable: a spoken query runs about **seven words against three for a typed one**,
and **73.4% of voice queries now start with a question word** — how, what, where. People are not
reciting keywords at their phones. They are asking.

Voice questions have a shape:

- **They’re full sentences.** Not keywords. Actual questions with “who,” “can,” “how much,” “is
  there.”
- **They’re simple.** One thing at a time. Nobody dictates a paragraph.
- **They’re specific.** A town, a day, a problem, a price. “Today.” “Near me.” “How much.”
- **They expect a spoken-style answer back.** Short. Direct. Sounds like a person.

When an AI answers a voice question, it’s often reading its answer out loud, or showing something
short enough to glance at. It can’t quote three paragraphs of “we pride ourselves on excellence.” It
needs a clean, quotable sentence that answers the exact thing that was asked.

If your site doesn’t have that sentence, the AI finds a business that does.

## This is a ten-year decision, not a Tuesday one

It’s tempting to treat AI search as a trend to check on later. Wait and see. Fix it when it’s bigger.

The problem is that the customers driving it aren’t waiting. Every year, another group of people
finishes school, starts earning, and starts asking AI where to spend that money. Every year, the
share of your market that “just asks” gets larger, and the share that “just Googles” gets smaller.
That isn’t a wave that passes. It’s a floor that keeps rising.

SEO isn’t going away. A solid website that ranks well is still the foundation, and it’s still what an
AI reads to learn about you. [AEO](/blog/what-is-aeo) — Answer Engine Optimization — is the next
floor up. It’s making sure that when someone asks a question you can answer, the AI has a clear,
readable, quotable answer from you to hand back.

Building that floor now, while most of your competitors haven’t, is the cheapest it will ever be.

## How to write for the customer who talks

You don’t have to rebuild your website. You have to change how a few key answers are written.

**Answer the spoken question, not the keyword.** Write the question the way someone would say it out
loud. “Do you do same-day repairs?” beats “Same-Day Repair Services.” Then answer it in the first
sentence.

**Keep the answer short enough to say.** Read your answer out loud. If you’d be embarrassed reading
it to a customer on the phone, an AI won’t quote it either. Aim for one or two plain sentences, then
details if needed.

**Put the specifics in the sentence.** Where, when, how much, how fast. “Yes — we do same-day repairs
in Portsmouth and Dover, Monday through Saturday, usually within four hours.” That sentence answers
five voice questions at once.

**Use the words people say, not the words you use.** Customers say “broken tooth,” not “dental
trauma.” They say “won’t turn on,” not “power fault.” Match their language and the AI can match you
to their question.

**Cover the boring questions too.** Hours, parking, whether you take cards, whether you come to them.
Younger customers ask these by voice constantly, and a clear answer wins the referral before anyone
compares prices.

**Make sure AI can actually read it.** This is the one that quietly sinks businesses. Most
[FAQ plugins and accordion widgets](/blog/why-faq-plugins-arent-a-good-idea) load their answers with
JavaScript, and AI crawlers generally don’t run JavaScript. Your answers can look perfect to a
visitor and be completely invisible to the engine doing the referring. They need to exist as plain,
crawlable HTML on your own website.
