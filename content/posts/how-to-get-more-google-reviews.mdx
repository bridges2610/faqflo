Ask a business owner what their reviews are for and most will say the same thing: “So people trust
us.” True. But that’s only half the job now.

When someone asks ChatGPT or Google’s AI “who’s a good roofer near me,” the answer doesn’t come from
a ranking. It comes from whatever the AI can read about you and believe. Your Google reviews are one
of the biggest piles of that evidence, and they’re written by people who aren’t you, which is exactly
why an AI takes them seriously.

So let’s cover two things. First, how to actually get more reviews without being weird about it.
Second, why those reviews are quietly doing AEO work for you, whether you planned it or not.

## First, get your Google Business Profile ready

Before you ask a single customer, make sure the profile they’re about to review is worth reviewing.

**Claim and verify it.** If you haven’t, go to your Google Business Profile and complete
verification. You can’t reply to reviews on a profile you don’t own.

**Fill in every field.** Hours, service area, categories, phone, website, photos. A half-empty
profile makes a reviewer hesitate and makes an AI unsure what you do.

**Grab your review link.** Google gives you a short link that drops the customer straight into the
review box — look for the option to ask for reviews in your profile dashboard. Save it somewhere you
can copy it in two seconds. This link is the whole game.

## How to ask for reviews (the part that actually works)

Most businesses have plenty of satisfied customers and very few reviews. The gap isn’t satisfaction.
It’s that nobody asked, or they asked at the wrong time, or they made it a chore.

**Ask at the peak moment.** For a service business, that’s when the job is done and the customer is
standing there saying thanks. For a store, it’s after the product arrives and they’ve had a few days
with it. For a professional service, it’s when the problem is solved, not when the invoice goes out.
Timing is about catching people when the work is fresh — not about picking who to ask. More on that
distinction below, because Google cares about it.

**Ask in person, then follow up by text.** “Would you mind leaving us a Google review? I’ll text you
the link right now.” Then actually text it while you’re still in the driveway. A verbal ask plus an
instant link converts far better than either one alone.

**Make it one tap.** Send the direct review link. Not “search for us on Google.” Not “go to our
website and find the button.” One link, one tap, review box open.

**Keep the message short and human.** Something like: “Hi Dana, thanks again for having us out today.
If you have 60 seconds, a Google review really helps a small business like ours: [link]. Thank you!”
No pitch, no pressure — and note that it doesn’t ask how they felt about the job first.

**Put the link everywhere a customer already is.** Email signatures. The bottom of invoices and
receipts. A QR code on the counter, the truck, the takeout bag, the thank-you card in the box. The
point is to lower the effort to almost zero.

**Give a nudge on what to say.** People freeze at a blank box. “If you mention what we did and which
town you’re in, it helps other folks find us” produces a much better review than “leave us a review.”
(This matters for AEO too, and we’ll get there.)

**Follow up once.** If they said yes and didn’t do it, one friendly reminder a few days later is
fine. Two is nagging.

**Make it a habit, not a campaign.** A burst of 30 reviews in one week followed by silence looks odd
to Google and to customers. A steady trickle, a few every week, looks like a real business with real
customers.

## What not to do

A few things that feel like shortcuts and end up costing you.

**Don’t pay for reviews or offer discounts for them.** It’s against Google’s policies and the reviews
tend to get filtered anyway.

**Don’t review-gate — and the bar is lower than you think.** Review gating means screening customers
by how happy you expect them to be, then only sending the review link to the ones you like the look
of. Google updated its Maps review policy in April 2026 and now actively enforces this: gated reviews
can be removed and the profile itself penalised. The bar is lower than most people realise — even
“if you enjoyed your experience, please leave us a review” can read as gating, because it quietly
excludes everyone who didn’t. Ask everyone, and ask the same way.

**Don’t write them yourself, or have your cousin do it.** Fake reviews get caught, and a profile full
of five-star reviews with no detail reads as fake to humans and machines alike.

**Don’t ignore the bad ones.** A calm, specific reply to a negative review is often more persuasive
than ten positive reviews, because it shows how you handle a problem.

## Reply to every review

This is the step most businesses skip, and it’s the cheapest one.

Reply to every review, good or bad, within a day or two. Thank people by name. Mention the specific
job or product. Keep it short.

Replying does three things. It tells the next customer you pay attention. It tells Google the profile
is active. And it gives you a chance to add the specific, factual details an AI can read: “Thanks,
Mike. Glad the same-day water heater replacement in Dover worked out.” Now that reply says what you
do and where you do it, in plain words, on a page everyone trusts.

## Why reviews matter for local SEO

Reviews have been a core local ranking signal for years. Google documents three factors behind local
results — relevance, distance and prominence — and says review count and review score feed directly
into prominence. Freshness isn’t something Google spells out the same way, but every local
practitioner will tell you a steady flow beats a stale pile.

That’s the foundation, and it still matters. But there’s a floor above it now.

## Why reviews matter for AEO

[AEO](/blog/what-is-aeo), or answer engine optimization, is about being the business an AI names when
someone asks a question you could answer. Here’s where reviews fit.

**An AI needs evidence, and reviews are evidence it didn’t have to take your word for.** Your website
says you’re reliable. Two hundred strangers saying “showed up on time, fixed it, didn’t upsell” is a
different kind of proof. Answer engines lean on outside sources when deciding who to recommend, and
your Google profile is one of the most consistently readable ones.

**Themes beat individual reviews.** This is the part worth being precise about, because it changes
what you ask for. Assistants generally don’t read all two hundred of your reviews — they work from
summaries, star ratings, and whatever snippets happen to be visible on pages they can crawl. So no
single glowing review is going to carry you. What surfaces is the *pattern*: if forty people
independently mention same-day service in Portsmouth, that becomes something an AI can say about you.
That’s why nudging customers to name the job and the town works — not because one sentence gets
lifted, but because forty of them build a theme.

**Recency signals you’re still in business.** AI answers favour businesses that look current. A
steady flow of new reviews says you’re open, active, and worth recommending this month, not three
years ago.

**Your replies are content too.** Every reply you write is text on a trusted page, in your voice,
with your details. Treat it that way.

**Reviews and your own answers work together.** Here’s the thing reviews can’t do alone: they’re
scattered, they’re written in customers’ words, and they don’t answer the questions people actually
type. “Do you offer financing?” “How fast can you get here?” “Do you cover my town?” Those need clear
answers on your own website, in plain HTML an AI can read. Reviews mostly help an AI *choose between*
businesses it already knows about — [they rarely get you into the conversation in the first
place](/blog/does-ai-use-my-google-reviews-how-reviews-affect-ai-recommendations). Your own answers
are what do that.

That’s the loop. Reviews prove you’re real. Your answers tell the AI exactly what you do. Together,
they make you the easy name to say.
