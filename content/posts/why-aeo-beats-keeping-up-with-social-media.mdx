Same hours, wildly different shelf life. One is a treadmill, the other is a filing cabinet.

<PostTldr>
This is not an argument for quitting social media. It is an argument about shelf life.

- **Three posts a week is roughly 50 hours a year**, and almost none of it is still working

- **Fifteen written answers is a few hours, once**, and it keeps answering long after.
- **Social is a retention channel dressed as an acquisition channel.** It is very good with

Nobody has ever told a small business owner to do less. The advice is always additive: post
more, post consistently, get on the new platform, do video now, do short video now, three times
a week, at the right time of day, with the right hook.

So let&rsquo;s do the thing nobody does and talk about where your limited hours actually go
furthest.

This isn&rsquo;t an argument for abandoning social media. It&rsquo;s an argument about shelf
life — and once you see the difference, it&rsquo;s hard to unsee.

## The treadmill

Here&rsquo;s the deal social media offers you.

You produce something. It&rsquo;s shown to some fraction of the people who already follow you,
for somewhere between a few hours and a few days. Then it&rsquo;s gone — not deleted, just
buried nineteen posts deep in a feed where nobody, including you, will ever find it again.

To keep the results, you have to keep producing. Stop for three weeks and the results stop with
you. That&rsquo;s not a flaw in how you&rsquo;re doing it; it&rsquo;s the actual design. The
platform&rsquo;s job is to keep a feed moving, not to keep your business findable.

Run the arithmetic on a realistic year:

- Three posts a week, fifty weeks — call it 150 posts
- Twenty minutes each, including the photo, the caption, and the deciding-what-to-post part
- **Roughly 50 hours**

At the end of that year, what&rsquo;s still working for you? Essentially nothing from month
one. Maybe a handful of posts that did unusually well. You&rsquo;re renting attention, and rent
is due weekly.

And here&rsquo;s the part that stings: you almost certainly answered a customer&rsquo;s pricing
question in a comment thread in March, clearly and well. That answer is now unreachable. It
exists, technically. It&rsquo;s findable by no one.

## The filing cabinet

Now the other option.

You write down the fifteen questions customers actually ask you before they book. What it
costs, how fast you can come, which towns you cover, whether there&rsquo;s a call-out fee,
what&rsquo;s included, how long the job takes, whether you&rsquo;re licensed and insured.

Two to four plain sentences each. You already know every answer — you say them out loud ten
times a day.

Then you put them on your own website as readable text.

- **Roughly 3 to 5 hours, once**

That page doesn&rsquo;t decay. It gets crawled, and re-crawled. It&rsquo;s still answering
questions in eighteen months. When an answer engine builds a response about your trade in your
town, that&rsquo;s the material it works from — and when it cites you, the credit and the call
come to you, because it&rsquo;s your domain.

Fifty hours of renting attention against four hours of building an asset. That&rsquo;s the
actual comparison, and almost nobody frames it that way because nobody sells you the four-hour
version.

## What social genuinely does well

I want to be fair here, because the &ldquo;quit social media&rdquo; version of this argument is
wrong and you&rsquo;d be right to distrust it.

Social is good at things AEO can&rsquo;t touch:

- **Showing your work.** Before-and-after photos are proof, and proof is persuasive.
- **Staying present with people who already know you.** Repeat customers, past clients, the
  local network.
- **Community.** A local group where you&rsquo;re a known, helpful presence is real business.
- **Personality.** People hire people, and a feed carries that in a way an FAQ page never will.

None of that is nothing. Keep doing it.

But notice what&rsquo;s not on that list: being discovered by someone who doesn&rsquo;t know you
exist. Social is fundamentally a retention and reputation channel dressed up as an acquisition
channel. The stranger with a leaking roof at 8pm is not scrolling your feed. They&rsquo;re
asking somebody — and increasingly that somebody is an AI assistant.

## Where the two collide

The awkward truth underneath all of this: most of what you post to social is invisible to
answer engines anyway.

Those platforms render nearly everything with JavaScript, much of it sits behind a login, and
crawlers generally don&rsquo;t run JavaScript or hold accounts. And when something is readable,
the source is the platform rather than your business — you didn&rsquo;t earn the citation, you
don&rsquo;t own the address, and the customer&rsquo;s path doesn&rsquo;t lead back to you.

So the content isn&rsquo;t just short-lived. For the purposes of getting recommended, a lot of
it was never counted at all.

Which means the 50 hours and the 4 hours aren&rsquo;t competing for the same outcome.
They&rsquo;re doing different jobs, and only one of them makes you findable by strangers.

## The order to do it in

You don&rsquo;t have to choose. You have to sequence.

1. **Write the answers once, on your own site.** Fifteen questions, plain sentences, real
   numbers, real town names. Answer in the first sentence — engines and skimming customers both
   reward it.

2. **Make sure a machine can actually read them.** Open your live page, right-click, choose View
   Page Source, press Ctrl+F, and search for one of your answers. If it isn&rsquo;t there,
   it&rsquo;s being built by JavaScript after the page loads and the engines will never see it.

3. **Then post, with less pressure.** Once the durable version exists, social goes back to being
   what it&rsquo;s good at. You&rsquo;re not trying to make a Tuesday post carry your pricing
   policy anymore.

4. **Harvest your own comments.** This is the highest-return hour in the whole plan. Go back
   through your replies from the last few months and pull out every question you answered well.
   That&rsquo;s your FAQ page, already written, in your own voice, by the person who actually
   knows the answers.

5. **Update it when reality changes**, and otherwise leave it alone. That&rsquo;s the entire
   maintenance burden. It&rsquo;s about an hour a quarter.

⚠️ Use View Page Source, not Inspect Element. Inspect shows the page after scripts have run,
which is what a person sees rather than what a crawler gets.

## The reframe

Social media asks: what will you publish this week?

AEO asks: what do people ask you, and is the answer written down anywhere a machine can read?

The first question never stops. The second one has an end. You finish it, and then it keeps
working — while you&rsquo;re on a job, while you&rsquo;re asleep, while you&rsquo;re taking the
week off, which is exactly when a feed goes quiet and stops producing anything at all.

And none of this is a departure from regular SEO. A clear, crawlable site that answers real
questions has always been the foundation, and it still is. Answer Engine Optimization is the
floor above it: writing those answers so they can be lifted cleanly into somebody else&rsquo;s
answer, and making sure they&rsquo;re actually readable when the engine comes looking.
