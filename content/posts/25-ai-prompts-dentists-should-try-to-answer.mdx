A patient with a broken tooth used to Google “emergency dentist near me” and start calling down the
list. Now a lot of them open ChatGPT and just ask. They get two or three practice names, a sentence
about each, and a reason to pick one. Then they call that one.

You never see this happen. There’s no click in your analytics, no search term in your reports.
Either your name came up or it didn’t.

The good news is you can check. Below are 25 prompts real patients use before they choose a dentist.
Run them yourself, see who gets named, then make sure your website actually answers each one.

## How to use this list

Open ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google. Paste in each prompt and swap `[city]` for your town or
neighborhood. Don’t sign in as yourself if you can help it — you want the answer a stranger gets,
not one shaped by your history.

Keep a simple sheet as you go. Three columns is enough:

## Emergency and urgent care

These are the highest-intent prompts there are. Nobody asks them casually, and the person asking will
call somebody within the hour.

- “Which dentist near me can see me today for a broken tooth?”
- “Who does emergency dental work on weekends in [city]?”
- “I chipped a tooth — is that an emergency?”
- “My crown fell out. What do I do until I can get to a dentist?”
- “How fast can I get seen for bad tooth pain in [city]?”

**To be the answer here**, your site needs to say in plain text whether you hold same-day slots, what
your after-hours number is, and how quickly you typically see emergencies. “Call us” is not an
answer an AI can quote.

## Cost and insurance

Money questions are where most practices go quiet, and silence is exactly why the AI names somebody
else.

- “How much does a dental crown cost in [city]?”
- “Which dentists near me take my insurance?”
- “What does a cleaning cost without insurance?”
- “Are there dentists in [city] with payment plans?”
- “Do any dentists near me have a new patient special?”

**To be the answer here**, put real numbers or real ranges on the page, name the plans you accept,
and describe your membership or financing option in a sentence. A range like “$1,100–$1,600
depending on material” is quotable. “Contact us for pricing” is not.

## Choosing a practice

This is the recommendation moment — the one that used to come from a neighbor.

- “Best family dentist in [city]”
- “Who’s a good dentist near me that’s taking new patients?”
- “What’s a good dentist in [city] for someone with dental anxiety?”
- “Are there dentists near me open early or on Saturdays?”
- “Which dentist near me is closest to [neighborhood]?”

**To be the answer here**, be specific about who you’re for and where you are. “Accepting new
patients”, “7am appointments Monday through Thursday”, and the neighborhoods you actually serve are
the details that get lifted straight into an answer.

## Specific procedures

Procedure prompts bring in the highest-value cases, and they’re the ones patients research hardest.

- “Who does Invisalign in [city] and what does it cost?”
- “Where can I get dental implants near me?”
- “Which dentists in [city] do same-day crowns?”
- “Do I need a root canal, or can it just be filled?”
- “Which dentists near me do teeth whitening that actually works?”

**To be the answer here**, name the procedures and the technology by name. If you have a milling unit
for same-day crowns, say so in words a patient understands, and say roughly what it costs and how
long it takes.

## Kids, fear and comfort

Soft questions with hard consequences. A parent who trusts your answer books the whole family.

- “At what age should my child first see a dentist?”
- “Which dentists near me are good with kids?”
- “Do any dentists in [city] offer sedation?”
- “Can my whole family get seen on the same day somewhere near me?”
- “Is it worth switching dentists if mine stopped taking my insurance?”

**To be the answer here**, answer the general question honestly first, then say what you do about it.
AI engines quote practices that teach before they sell.

## What you’ll probably find

Most dentists who run this exercise see one of three things.

**A directory got named instead of you.** Yelp, Healthgrades, or a “best dentists in [city]”
listicle. The AI had to recommend something, and a directory was the readable option.

**A competitor got named.** Usually not the best practice in town — the one with the clearest, most
specific content about hours, pricing and services.

**Nobody local got named at all.** You get generic advice about what to do for a broken tooth and no
names. That’s an open field, and whoever fills it first wins it.

None of these mean you’re a worse dentist. They mean the engine couldn’t find anything solid to say
about you.

While you’re looking, check the source links under the answer as well as the names in it. Those are
[two different wins](/blog/what-it-means-to-be-cited-versus-named-in-ai), and being named without
being linked means the AI is describing your practice from somebody else’s page.

## Why your FAQ page probably isn’t helping

Here’s the part that catches almost everyone.

Most dental websites have an FAQ section, and most of them are built with
[a plugin or an accordion widget](/blog/why-faq-plugins-arent-a-good-idea) that loads its answers
with JavaScript. A patient taps a question, it expands, the answer appears. Looks great.

AI crawlers generally don’t run JavaScript. They read the raw HTML of your page and move on. So the
answers you already wrote — the ones that would have won you the citation — aren’t in the version
the crawler sees. Your FAQ page is invisible to the exact engines you’re trying to reach.

Your answers have to exist as real, crawlable HTML on your own domain. Same words, different
plumbing, completely different result. There’s a
[fuller walkthrough for dental practices here](/blog/how-dentists-can-win-with-ai).

## What a quotable answer looks like

Take the crown cost question.

**Not quotable:** “We offer high-quality crowns using the latest technology. Our team is committed to
beautiful, lasting results. Contact us today for a consultation.”

**Quotable:** “A dental crown at our Boise office runs $1,150 to $1,500 depending on the material.
Same-day porcelain crowns are milled in-house and usually done in one two-hour visit. Most PPO plans
cover 40 to 50 percent.”

The second one has a price, a place, a timeframe and an insurance detail. An answer engine can lift a
sentence of that straight into a response and cite you. The first one has nothing to grab.

Answer-first, factual, specific. That’s the whole formula.
