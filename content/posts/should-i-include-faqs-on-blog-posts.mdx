The short answer is yes, and it’s one of the easiest improvements available to most blogs. But
the reason to do it has changed, and a lot of the advice floating around is still arguing for a
payoff that no longer exists.

So let’s be specific about why, when, and how many — and about when the answer is actually no.

## The reason people used to do it

For years, the pitch was Google’s FAQ rich results: those expandable question-and-answer
dropdowns that appeared under your listing, taking up more space and pushing competitors down the
page.

Google retired those for the overwhelming majority of sites. If that was your reason, your reason
is gone, and no amount of markup brings it back.

Plenty of blogs stopped adding FAQs at that point, which was a reasonable response to the
information available. It also happened to be exactly the wrong moment to stop.

## The better reason to do it now

Answer engines — ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google’s AI Overviews — build responses by pulling clean,
self-contained chunks of text out of pages. A question with a direct two-to-three sentence answer
underneath is close to the ideal shape for that. It’s unambiguous, it’s short enough to lift
whole, and it announces what it’s about in its own first line.

Most blog posts are the opposite shape. They’re an argument that unfolds over 1,200 words, where
the useful answer is embedded in paragraph nine and depends on paragraphs one through eight for
context. That’s good writing. It’s also hard to extract cleanly.

An FAQ block at the end of a post gives you both: the piece for the reader who wants the whole
argument, and the extractable chunks for the reader — human or machine — who wants the answer.

There’s a second, less discussed benefit. Writing the FAQ forces you to notice what your post
didn’t answer. If you can’t write five real questions your article addresses, the article is
probably vaguer than you thought.

## The four conditions

Here’s where most FAQ blocks go wrong.

**1. They have to be real questions.**

“What makes our approach unique?” is not a question. Nobody typed it. It’s a marketing prompt
wearing a question mark, and it makes the whole block read as filler.

Real questions come from real places: what people ask you in email, what shows up in your support
inbox, what related searches Google suggests, what an AI gives you if you ask “what do people
usually ask about [topic].” If you wouldn’t hear it out loud from a customer, cut it.

**2. They can’t just repeat the article.**

If your FAQ answer is a compressed version of a paragraph the reader just read, you’ve added
length without adding value. The best FAQ questions are adjacent — the natural follow-ups. The
post explains what something is; the FAQs handle “how much does it cost,” “how long does it
take,” “does it work for my situation,” “what’s the difference between this and that.”

Think of it as the conversation that would happen right after someone finished reading.

**3. The answers have to be answers.**

Two to three sentences. Direct in the first one. Specific.

“It depends on a number of factors” is not an answer, and it’s the single most common thing that
ruins an otherwise good FAQ block. If it depends, say what it depends on and give the range.

**4. It has to be readable by machines.**

This is the one that quietly undoes everything else, and it catches a lot of people who did the
first three properly.

Most FAQ plugins and accordion widgets build their content with JavaScript after the page loads.
AI crawlers generally don’t run JavaScript — they fetch the raw HTML and move on. So the block
looks perfect to your readers and does not exist for the engines you added it for.

Check it in twenty seconds: open the live post, right-click, choose View Page Source, then press
Ctrl+F and search for a phrase from one of your answers. If it’s not in there, it’s not in the
page.

⚠️ Use View Page Source, not Inspect Element. Inspect shows you the page after scripts have run,
which is what a person sees rather than what a crawler gets.

If your FAQs fail that check, the fix is putting them in as plain HTML rather than through a
widget. Every major builder has a native code or HTML block — we walked through all four
[step by step here](/blog/how-to-add-html-to-wordpress-squarespace-webflow-or-wix). On Wix, use
native text elements, since its embed tool wraps content in an iframe that crawlers treat as a
separate document.

## How many, and where

**Five is a good number.** Enough to cover the real follow-ups, few enough that you’re not
padding. If you can only think of three genuine ones, publish three.

**Put them at the end.** After the article, before any final call to action. The reader who wanted
the argument has finished it; the reader who scrolled straight down gets the quick answers.

**Use a real heading**, like “Frequently Asked Questions,” with each question as a subheading and
the answer as plain text underneath. Simple structure is easier for everything to parse —
including a person skimming on a phone.

**Don’t reuse the same block across posts.** A boilerplate FAQ pasted onto twenty articles is
duplicate content, adds nothing to any of them, and signals low effort. Each post gets questions
specific to that post.

## When the answer is no

Skip the FAQ block if:

- **The post is already a Q&A.** A post structured entirely as questions doesn’t need a second
  set at the bottom.
- **You can’t think of five real questions.** Three good ones beat five with two inventions. Zero
  good ones means the block shouldn’t be there.
- **It’s a personal, narrative, or news post.** An announcement or a story doesn’t have follow-up
  questions in this sense, and bolting some on reads as strange.
- **You’d only be doing it for rich results.** That reason is gone. Doing it for extractable
  answers is a good reason; doing it out of habit isn’t.

## What about schema?

Add it if it’s easy, and don’t overthink it.

FAQPage markup, rendered server-side into the page source rather than injected by a script, makes
it unambiguous which text answers which question. That’s genuinely useful for machine parsing.

What it won’t do is cause AI citations. No answer engine has published a rule that rewards FAQ
markup, and anyone telling you it’s the switch that turns on AI visibility is overselling it.
Schema is a label on a box — useful when there’s something in the box, useless when there isn’t.

One rule that still matters: don’t mark up content that isn’t visible on the page. Schema
describing hidden answers is against Google’s guidelines and can earn a manual penalty. The
markup describes the page; it doesn’t replace it.

## A note on how we do it

Nearly every article on this blog ends with five FAQs, two to three sentences each, specific to
that post. That isn’t a coincidence, and it isn’t a template we apply mechanically — it’s the
format we’d recommend, applied to our own content, which seems like the minimum bar for writing
about it.

The exceptions are the ones this post would predict. A couple of the oldest explainers run to
seven or nine, from before we settled on five. And two posts have none at all, because they’re
the case where the honest answer is to skip the block rather than invent questions to fill it.

If you go back through your own archive, retrofitting the good posts is usually a couple of hours
of work for a meaningful improvement. Start with your highest-traffic posts and the ones closest
to a purchase decision.

And none of this is a departure from ordinary SEO. A well-structured page that clearly answers
real questions has been good practice for as long as there’s been search. Answer Engine
Optimization is the floor above that foundation: writing those answers so they can be lifted
cleanly into somebody else’s answer, and making sure they’re actually there to be read when an
engine arrives.
