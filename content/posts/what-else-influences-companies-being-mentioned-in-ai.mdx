Your website is only one of the places AI looks. Here’s the rest of the room — and why it matters.

<PostTldr>
AI answers are assembled from several sources at once, and your website is only one of them.

- Your Google Business Profile is the single most important one for a local business.
- Reviews do two jobs: they build confidence, and they hand the AI the words it uses to describe
  you.
- Directories, “best of” lists and community threads get quoted constantly — often ahead of any
  business’s own website.
- Consistency is the quiet decider. Different phone numbers in different places make you look
  unverified.
- You control your site completely, your listings mostly, and everything else only by earning it.

</PostTldr>

You fixed your website. The answers are in plain HTML, the crawlers are allowed in, the FAQ page
finally says what customers actually ask. Good. That’s the part you control most directly, and most
businesses never do it.

But you can do all of that and still watch an AI name somebody else.

That’s because an answer engine doesn’t just read your site and repeat it. It gathers information
about you from several places, sees whether those places agree, and then decides how confident it
feels naming you to a stranger. Your website is one voice in that room. It’s the one with the most
obvious reason to say nice things about you, so it rarely gets the final word on its own.

Here’s what else is in the room.

## Why AI looks past your website at all

Put yourself in the AI’s position for a second.

Someone asks it for a good roofer in your town. It has to name one or two businesses to a person
it’s never met, and it will get blamed if the answer is wrong. So it does what a careful person
does: it checks whether more than one source says the same thing.

Your website says you do emergency roof repair and you’re open Saturdays. Fine — but every roofing
website says something like that. When your Google Business Profile also says Saturday hours, and
forty reviews mention someone showing up on a weekend, and a local directory lists you under roofing
in the right town, the AI stops guessing. Now it has agreement, and agreement is what confidence is
made of.

This is why a business with a plain website and a strong everything-else often gets named ahead of a
business with a gorgeous website and nothing around it. A competitor doesn’t have to be better than
you. They just have to be easier to verify than you.

Read that the right way round, though. It isn’t an argument that your website doesn’t matter — it’s
an argument about what your website is *for*. The AI is checking sources against each other, and
your site is one of the sources being checked. A site that looks expensive but never states your
hours, your service area or your prices in words a crawler can read isn’t a weak source. It’s an
absent one. There’s nothing on it to agree with, so you’ve handed the AI one fewer confirmation at
the exact moment it was looking for one.

And it’s the only source in the room that speaks in your own words about the specifics. Your Google
listing can confirm you’re open Saturdays. It isn’t going to explain what your warranty covers, why
you don’t do flat roofs, or what you charge to come out. Design isn’t what gets checked. Answers
are.

## The big one: your Google Business Profile

If you only do one thing after reading this, do this one.

Your Google Business Profile is the free listing that shows up on the right side of Google and on
Google Maps — your name, hours, phone, categories, service area, photos, and reviews. It’s the most
complete, most current, most structured description of your business that exists anywhere, and it
wasn’t written by your marketing.

That makes it the first place AI systems look for local answers. Google’s own AI features read that
data directly, because Google owns it — when Google or Gemini answers a “best X near me” question,
your profile is the backbone of what it has to work with. An empty profile means there’s not much to
recommend.

The part people miss is that it also reaches the AI tools that have nothing to do with Google. Your
profile data spreads. It flows into map apps, aggregators, directory listings, and pages all over
the web that pull business details from it — and those pages are crawlable by everybody. ChatGPT
doesn’t read Google’s results directly, but the sites it can read are full of information that came
from your profile, so a strong profile reaches it the long way round.

What “complete” actually means, in plain terms:

- **The right primary category.** This is the biggest single lever on the profile. “Plumber” and
  “septic system service” are different businesses to a machine.
- **Every service listed.** If it isn’t listed, you don’t do it as far as the AI is concerned.
- **Hours that are true, including holidays.** Wrong hours are worse than no hours — they teach the
  systems that your data is unreliable, and they
  [end up quoted back to your customers](/blog/ai-is-telling-customers-the-wrong-hours-about-your-business).
- **Service area set correctly** if you travel to customers.
- **Recent photos.** Recency reads as “this business is still operating.”
- **Reviews that get replies.** More on that next.

A large share of local businesses have never claimed or filled this profile out at all — which is
either depressing or an opportunity, depending on which side of it you’re on.

## Reviews: they do two separate jobs

Everyone knows reviews matter. Most people only know half of why.

Job one is confidence. A steady stream of recent, genuine reviews tells the AI you’re a real, active
business that people keep hiring. A profile with six reviews from 2021 reads as dormant.

Job two is language, and this is the part nobody talks about. When an AI recommends you, it doesn’t
just list your name — it says why. “They’re known for showing up same day and not pushing upsells.”
Where did that sentence come from? Your reviews. The AI read them, spotted the themes, and turned
them into its description of you.

That means your reviews are effectively writing your sales pitch. If forty customers mention that
you explain things clearly, “explains things clearly” becomes what the AI tells strangers about you.
If your reviews are all four-word “great job” reviews, the AI has nothing specific to say, and it
will describe a competitor instead — because that competitor’s reviews gave it something to work
with.

You can’t script reviews. You can ask for them at the right moment, and you can reply to them, which
adds more text and shows the profile is tended. There’s more on what AI does and doesn’t take from
them in [does AI use my Google reviews](/blog/does-ai-use-my-google-reviews-how-reviews-affect-ai-recommendations).

## The other sites AI actually pulls from

Here’s the uncomfortable pattern in the citation research: when AI engines answer “who’s the best X
near me,” the sources they name are frequently not business websites at all. They’re third-party
sites — review platforms, directories, ranking posts, and forums.

Take the specific numbers in that research with a grain of salt. A lot of it is published by
companies selling AI visibility services, the figures move around between studies, and no single
site dominates — even the most-quoted domain on any engine accounts for a small slice of all
citations. But the *shape* holds up across independent studies, and it matches how the answers
actually read. So here’s what’s in the room, and what each one is doing there.

**Review platforms** — Yelp, Google reviews, Trustpilot, the Better Business Bureau, Angi. Why AI
trusts them: independent, structured, hard to fake at scale, full of first-hand customer language.
ChatGPT in particular blends in outside providers like Yelp and TripAdvisor rather than relying on
what a business says about itself. These are the closest thing an AI has to asking around.

**Directories and “best of” lists** — local roundups, city guides, “top 10 plumbers in [city]”
posts, chamber of commerce listings. Why AI trusts them: somebody already did the ranking. An AI
asked for a shortlist loves finding a shortlist. This is why those thin-looking “best HVAC companies
in Tampa” blog posts punch far above their weight — they’re built in exactly the shape the question
is asked in.

**Reddit and community threads.** Why AI trusts them: it’s real people arguing about who’s good and
who ripped them off, which is very close to what the person asking actually wants. Study after study
through 2026 has put Reddit at or near the top of the most-cited domains across the major engines.
You can’t manufacture this, and you shouldn’t try — astroturfing a local subreddit is a good way to
become the cautionary tale in the thread. But a customer saying your name in a “who should I call”
thread is worth more than most things you could buy. We went deeper on that in
[Reddit beats your website in AI answers](/blog/reddit-beats-your-website-in-ai-answers-heres-why-that-should-worry-you).

**Industry-specific directories.** Healthgrades and Zocdoc for medical, Avvo and Justia for law,
Houzz and Angi for contractors, TripAdvisor and OpenTable for restaurants, Psychology Today for
therapists. Why AI trusts them: they’re specialists. For a question about dentists, a dentist
directory carries more weight than a general one. These are the most under-used listings in small
business, and they’re usually free.

**Bing.** Why it matters: Bing’s index was the original engine behind ChatGPT’s live search and it
still feeds into it, along with OpenAI’s own crawler and a handful of other providers. So Bing isn’t
the whole story it was a year ago — but a page that appears nowhere in Bing has one fewer route in,
and Copilot leans on the same index. Claiming a free Bing Places listing takes about fifteen
minutes, and almost nobody bothers.

**Social profiles and YouTube** — Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, a YouTube channel. Why AI uses
them: mostly as confirmation that you exist, are active, and are who you say you are. LinkedIn does
heavy lifting for B2B specifically. YouTube is cited heavily by Google’s AI answers, which matters
if you’re in a trade where people search “how to” before they search “who can.”

**Local news, local blogs, and any press.** Why AI trusts them: a third party with no stake wrote
your name down. Sponsoring the little league team and getting a line in the local paper is an
[AEO](/blog/what-is-aeo) tactic now, which is funny but true.

**Wikipedia and Wikidata.** Why it matters: mostly for larger brands. If you’re a five-person
plumbing company, you don’t need a Wikipedia page and you’re not getting one. Skip it.

## The invisible one: consistency

This is the least exciting item on the list and one of the most decisive.

Your business name, address, and phone number need to match everywhere they appear. Same name — not
“Smith Plumbing” in one place and “Smith Plumbing & Heating LLC” in another. Same phone. Same
address, formatted the same way. Old listings from a previous address or a disconnected number are
actively working against you.

Here’s why a machine cares so much about something so boring. An AI is trying to decide whether all
these mentions refer to one business. Every mismatch is a reason to think they might be two
businesses, or one business with unreliable data. Neither makes it more likely to say your name out
loud.

There’s no clever version of this. It’s an afternoon with a spreadsheet, searching your own business
name and fixing what’s wrong.

## What you can and can’t control

Being straight about this, because plenty of people will sell you the opposite.

**Fully in your control:** your website content, whether it’s crawlable, your Google Business
Profile, your listings on directories and Bing, your name/address/phone consistency, and asking
happy customers for reviews.

**Partly in your control:** what your reviews say — you influence it by doing good work and asking
at the right time — whether local press mentions you, and whether you show up on best-of lists.

**Not in your control:** Reddit threads, what any individual AI decides to do on any given day, and
how these systems weight their sources, which changes without warning and differs between engines.
Citation patterns vary enough from platform to platform that no single checklist guarantees
visibility everywhere.

Anyone promising you guaranteed AI mentions is selling something they can’t deliver. What you can
actually do is make yourself the easiest business in your market to verify, and let that do the
work.

## The order to do it in

If you’ve got a few hours total, spend them like this.

1. **Claim and complete your Google Business Profile.** Biggest return, costs nothing.
2. **Fix your name, address, and phone everywhere.** Kill the dead listings.
3. **Get your recent review count up** and reply to the ones you have.
4. **Claim Bing Places** and the two or three directories that matter in your trade.
5. **Make sure your own site actually answers the questions people ask** — in plain, crawlable HTML
   that doesn’t need JavaScript to appear.

That last one is the one that fails silently, so here’s how to check it.

**The 20-second check:** open your FAQ page, right-click, choose View Page Source, then press Ctrl+F
(or Cmd+F) and search for a sentence from one of your answers. If it’s there, AI crawlers can read
it. If it isn’t, your answers are being drawn in by JavaScript after the page loads — which means
visitors see them and [AI crawlers don’t](/blog/why-faq-plugins-arent-a-good-idea).
