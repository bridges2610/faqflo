Open almost any small business FAQ page and you&rsquo;ll find some version of this:

*What makes you different? Are you committed to quality? What is your mission?*

Nobody has ever typed those into anything. They&rsquo;re not questions — they&rsquo;re marketing
prompts wearing a question mark.

Meanwhile the things customers actually ask, ten times a day, on the phone, are nowhere on the
page.

Fixing that is one of the highest-return afternoons available to a small business right now,
because those questions are exactly what people type into AI assistants — and an answer engine
can only recommend you if something readable answers the question it was asked.

## The three-source method for finding your real questions

You don&rsquo;t need keyword tools. You need to write down what&rsquo;s already happening.

**1. Your phone.** For one week, keep a note open and jot down every question a customer asks
*before* they book. Not after — before. Those are buying questions, and they&rsquo;re the ones
you want on the page. You&rsquo;ll have fifteen by Wednesday and they&rsquo;ll repeat heavily.

**2. Your inbox.** Search your email for question marks. Look at what people ask in a first
message. Same for your contact form submissions, your DMs, and your text messages.

**3. Ask an AI what people ask.** Genuinely useful and takes two minutes: &ldquo;What do people
usually ask before hiring a [your trade] in [your town]?&rdquo; You&rsquo;ll get a solid starter
list, and you can sanity-check it against what you know.

Then ask the assistants the customer questions themselves and see who gets named. Whoever keeps
coming up has a website that answers something yours doesn&rsquo;t.

## The questions almost every business should answer

There&rsquo;s a core set that applies across nearly every local or service business. If yours
doesn&rsquo;t cover these, start here.

**Money**

- What does [common service] cost, or what&rsquo;s the typical range?
- Do you charge a call-out, diagnostic, or consultation fee — and is it waived if I book?
- Do you offer financing or payment plans?
- Do you give free estimates?

**Availability and speed**

- How soon can you come out?
- Do you offer emergency or same-day service?
- What are your hours, and do you work weekends?
- Is there an after-hours or weekend surcharge?

**Where**

- Which towns and neighborhoods do you serve?
- Do you travel outside that area, and is there a fee?

**Scope**

- What exactly do you do?
- What don&rsquo;t you do?
- Do you handle [the specific thing customers always ask about]?

**Trust**

- Are you licensed and insured?
- Do you guarantee your work, and for how long?
- How long have you been doing this?

**Process**

- What happens on the first visit?
- How long does the job usually take?
- Do I need to be home?
- How do I pay?

That&rsquo;s more than most businesses need to publish at once. Pick the ten your customers
actually ask most and answer those properly rather than covering all twenty badly.

## The four tests for a good FAQ question

Before something goes on the page, check it against these.

**Is it phrased the way a person would say it?** &ldquo;Do you cover Lakewood?&rdquo; not
&ldquo;Service Area Information.&rdquo; Match the words in someone&rsquo;s head, because those
are the words they type.

**Does it have a real answer?** If your answer is &ldquo;it depends,&rdquo; either give the
ranges and what they depend on, or leave the question off. A non-answer is worse than silence —
it wastes the one chance you had to be specific.

**Would answering it honestly ever cost you a job?** If yes, that&rsquo;s usually a sign it
should be on there. &ldquo;We have a $250 minimum&rdquo; filters out the customer who was going
to be annoyed anyway, and it makes every other answer on the page more credible.

**Is it something a customer would ask, or something you&rsquo;d like them to think?**
&ldquo;What makes you different from other roofers?&rdquo; is the second kind. Cut it.

## The four to actually skip

**Mission and values questions.** Put that on your About page if you want it. It has no place in
a set of answers.

**Anything that&rsquo;s really a sales pitch.** &ldquo;Why should I choose you?&rdquo; is a
pitch. If it&rsquo;s genuinely useful, rewrite it as a real question: &ldquo;Do you do the work
yourselves or subcontract it?&rdquo;

**Questions with no answer.** See above.

**Questions you invented for keywords.** If nobody has ever asked it, it doesn&rsquo;t help you,
and it dilutes the questions that do.

## How to write the answers

Format matters as much as content, because you&rsquo;re writing for two readers: a customer
skimming, and a machine extracting.

**Answer in the first sentence.** No preamble. &ldquo;Yes — we cover Lakewood, Golden, and
Arvada at no extra charge.&rdquo; Then any detail after.

**Two to four sentences.** Engines pull chunks; a tight chunk gets used and a long paragraph gets
skipped. Customers skim, and the same brevity wins there too.

**Real numbers, real place names, real timeframes.** &ldquo;Fast service&rdquo; is unquotable.
&ldquo;Most calls booked within 24 hours&rdquo; is a sentence an AI can lift directly into an
answer.

**No hedging you don&rsquo;t need.** &ldquo;Prices may vary depending on many factors&rdquo; adds
nothing. If it varies, say what it varies with and give the range.

**Update it when reality changes.** An FAQ with last year&rsquo;s prices actively harms you — a
customer who was told $89 and gets charged $129 is a bad start.

## Then make sure a machine can actually read it

This is the step that quietly undoes everything else.

Most FAQ plugins and accordion widgets build their answers with JavaScript after the page loads.
AI crawlers generally don&rsquo;t run JavaScript — they fetch the raw HTML and move on. So your
perfect, specific, hard-won answers are visible to visitors and completely absent for the engines
you wrote them for.

Check it in twenty seconds: open your live page, right-click, choose View Page Source, then press
Ctrl+F and search for one of your answers. If it&rsquo;s not there, it&rsquo;s not in the page.

⚠️ Use View Page Source, not Inspect Element. Inspect shows you the page after scripts have run,
which is what a person sees rather than what a crawler gets.

The fix is putting the questions and answers in as plain HTML. Every major builder has a native
code or HTML block for it — we walked through all four
[step by step here](/blog/how-to-add-html-to-wordpress-squarespace-webflow-or-wix). On Wix, use
native text elements instead, since its embed tool wraps content in an iframe that crawlers treat
as a separate document.

And none of this is a departure from ordinary SEO — a clear, crawlable page that answers real
questions has always been good for search. Answer Engine Optimization is the floor above that
foundation: writing those answers so they can be lifted cleanly into somebody else&rsquo;s
answer, and making sure they&rsquo;re actually readable when the engine arrives.
